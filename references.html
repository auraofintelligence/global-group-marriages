Sky and origin traditions

These accounts tell different stories about where people came from, who moved between sky and land, and what was passed on to later generations. The public sources introduce the tellers and the traditions in their own settings.

Country and sky

  • Wardaman creation ancestors

    Wardaman knowledge holder Bill Yidumduma Harney recounts how Dungdung made land and married Nardi, who came down from the sky. Their children became the lightning people. The account also passes on songs, law and responsibilities for Country.

    Read Harney's public account
  • Seven Sisters songlines

    In the Aboriginal-led exhibition developed with Martu, Ngaanyatjarra and Aṉangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara custodians, the sisters travel across Country before rising into the sky as the Pleiades.

    Read the National Museum of Australia account
  • Wandjina in the west Kimberley

    The Mowanjum community brings together Woddordda, Ngarinyin and Wunambal Gambera people. The centre describes Wandjina as a sacred creative force connected to the land, with law and imagery held by custodians.

    Read the Mowanjum Arts and Culture Centre account
  • Tagai in the Torres Strait

    Artist Gail Mabo shares the tradition passed on by her father Eddie Koiki Mabo. The Southern Cross is Tagai's left hand and helps guide island navigation and seasonal activities.

    Read Gail Mabo's account

Creation accounts

  • Egyptian Khnum

    Accounts of the ram-headed creator Khnum depict him shaping life on a potter's wheel. At Deir el-Bahri, a temple relief shows him forming Hatshepsut and her ka.

    See the Glencairn Museum account
  • Greek Prometheus

    In the Library attributed to Apollodorus, Prometheus shapes people from earth and water and brings them fire hidden from Zeus.

    Read the passage at Tufts Perseus
  • K’iche’ Maya Popol Wuj

    Heart of Sky and other creators make people from white and yellow maize. K’iche’ day keeper Juana Batz Puac describes maize as a living connection with the ancestors.

    Read the Smithsonian account
  • Norse Askr and Embla

    In Snorri's Gylfaginning, the sons of Borr find two trees by the sea, shape them into Askr and Embla, and give them life and human abilities.

    Read the translated passage hosted by the University of Washington
  • Yorùbá Ọbàtálá

    In a creation account centred on Ilé-Ifẹ̀, Ọbàtálá shapes people from clay. The role and details vary across tellings.

    Read the Metropolitan Museum of Art account
  • Sumerian Enki and Ninmaḫ

    Namma, with Ninmaḫ and the birth goddesses, works from Enki's plan to create a being from clay so the gods can share out their work.

    Read the Sumerian text in translation
  • Mesopotamian Anunna and Anunnaki

    These names describe groups of deities in Sumerian and Akkadian sources. Which gods are meant and how they relate to heaven, the underworld and particular cities varies with the text.

    Read the Ancient Mesopotamian Gods and Goddesses entry
  • Māori Tāne and Hineahuone

    In one Māori account, Tāne forms Hineahuone from the soil at Kurawaka. Their daughter Hinetītama is associated with the threshold between night and day.

    Read Rāwiri Taonui's account in Te Ara

Lost lands and modern visitors

  • Atlantis

    Plato's Timaeus places the island beyond the Pillars of Heracles and describes its loss after earthquakes and floods. Critias adds Poseidon, the mortal Cleito and the concentric rings of land and water.

    Read Timaeus · Read Critias
  • Lemuria

    Philip Lutley Sclater proposed a former land connection in 1864 to explain the distribution of animals including lemurs. Later writers developed the name into a lost homeland across several different traditions.

    Read Sclater's account of the name · Read about Sumathi Ramaswamy's history
  • Nordic visitors in modern UFO folklore

    In his account of a 1952 desert encounter, George Adamski described Orthon as human-looking, with sandy shoulder-length hair. The observatory illustration on Possible Futures draws on this visual tradition and the Nordics named in Luke's prospectus.

    Read the Denver Public Library account
